    What the research says

    Simulate the fight, not the winner

    Holmes, McHale and Żychaluk built a Markov chain model that plays each MMA contest out as a fight, estimating each fighter's skills from free public data, and compared it with bookmakers' odds in the International Journal of Forecasting (2023). This engine borrows the idea at a smaller scale; it is my build, not theirs.

    IJF abstract

    Markets are hard to beat

    Across 6,190 UFC fights from 2007 to 2022, betting odds showed no systematic bias: the calibration slope's 95% interval ran from 0.98 to 1.08. Favorites won about two-thirds of the time at Pinnacle from 2019 to 2021, and flat-betting every favorite, or every underdog, lost money once the margin was paid.

    AgentMMA summary of the audits

    Ratings top out in the high 60s

    FightMatrix found that no rating system it tested, Elo and Glicko included, picks winners much better than the high 60s percent. UFCalendar reports its own index picks the favorite 63% of the time with a Brier score of 0.232 over 26,261 fights, self-reported.

    FightMatrix · UFCalendar

    Judges score damage first

    The ABC criteria rank damage, then dominance, then duration; a 10–8 needs significant damage, and control without damage doesn't count. A study of UFC round scoring found head strikes matter most, knockdowns are huge, a takedown is worth about 50 seconds of control, and a submission attempt more than twice a takedown.

    ABC criteria · UFC judging analysis

    Every judge has a taste

    Bayesian hierarchical models of MMA judging show individual judges hold their own preferences for different actions, and those preferences can decide a bout (European Journal of Operational Research, 2024). The engine gives each simulated judge a personal weight on power and on grappling.

    EJOR abstract

    Close fights go long

    In 2,941 UFC fights, matchups priced between 30% and 70% ended by KO/TKO about 30% of the time and by unanimous decision about 40%; lopsided ones finished more often. Underdogs over-performed in close fights, but not reliably enough to bet on.

    Aggression to the Mean

    How the engine works

    Each simulated fight is a continuous-time Markov chain. At any moment the fighters are at distance, in the clinch, or one is on top. Landed strikes, clinch entries and breaks, takedown attempts, stand-ups and submission attempts each arrive at their own rate, so the time to the next event is random, the way it is in a real fight.

    Landing rates average one man's strikes landed per minute with the other's strikes absorbed, then scale by 0.85, because that average overshot elite fights in the backtest. Each landed strike picks a target from the fighter's real split. Head strikes carry knockdown risk based on career knockdown rates, pulled toward the league average so a short career doesn't read as cannon power, and rising with the defender's accumulated damage, fatigue and a bad leg. Some knockdowns end it; others leave a man rocked for 25 seconds. A shot can also wobble him without dropping him.

    Leg kicks cost mobility: a slower man throws less, gets hit more and can't sit down on punches, and enough of them can end a fight. Body shots drain the gas tank. Volkanovski's pace climbs by round and Gaethje's fades. Takedown odds combine the shooter's accuracy with the defender's takedown defense. On the ground, the top man strikes and hunts submissions until the bottom man stands, which takes Gaethje about 40 seconds and Volkanovski about 35.

    Three judges score each round on damage first: head strikes count most, then body, ground and leg strikes, with knockdowns, wobbles, takedowns, control time and submission attempts weighted from the scoring research. Each judge has a personal taste for power and for grappling, plus shared and individual noise. Corners can stop a fight between rounds when the damage piles up. And no one shows up as his exact career average: every simulated fight draws each man's output, power and chin for the night.

    Judgment calls, all on the knobs

    At 155 the baseline keeps 95% of Volkanovski's output and 85% of his power, and makes him 15% easier to drop than his featherweight self. Gaethje's leg-kick share starts at 18%, between his career 28% and the 6% he threw at Pimblett. Volkanovski shoots three takedowns per 15 minutes, Gaethje one and a half. Judges credit power modestly by default.
